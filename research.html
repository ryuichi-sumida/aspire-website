Our Research

Four research threads on how Deaf people actually sign to each other — repair, improvisation, cross-signing, and what AI would need to recognize any of this.

Self-initiated Repair (SIR)

Watch a Deaf signer mid-conversation and you'll see them stop, back up, and redo a sign — the “wait, let me try that again” move. Conversation analysts call this self-initiated repair, and it's one of the clearest windows we have into how signers monitor each other's understanding in real time.

Interactional dynamics in sign language.

When one signer doesn't share a vocabulary item with another, the repair often opens a small side conversation — a quick question-and-answer pair where the two work the meaning out together, drawing on signs, gesture, fingerspelling, and mouthing as one integrated resource.

Improvisational Signing

When two Deaf people meet without a shared sign language — or share one but hit an unfamiliar topic — they don't stall. They improvise: pulling from gesture, mime, spatial setup, drawing in the air. The speed and fluency of this improvisation is what we're trying to understand.

Example of sign language interaction.

This flexibility is particularly pronounced in Japan, where Deaf signers routinely adapt to environments — public services, classrooms, hospitals — that weren't designed with them in mind.

Online Cross-signing

Deaf people from different countries routinely make themselves understood to each other within minutes — a well-documented phenomenon in the sign linguistics literature (Zeshan, 2015). ASPIRE tracks exactly how this ad hoc communication gets built, from the opening seconds onward.

Case Study: UK and Japan
Numerical expressions came through a little more successfully with our Japanese participants, but signers from both regions bridged the gaps quickly — weaving gesture, International Sign, and spatial drawing into conversation as the situation demanded.

Dialogic Sign Language Recognition (DSLR)

The gap. Existing sign-language datasets are almost entirely monologic — one signer facing a camera, with no interlocutor. Real signing isn't that.

What we're building. We're pushing recognition technology past the monologue. The shift is from Continuous Sign Language Recognition (CSLR), which handles a single signer's stream, to Dialogic Sign Language Recognition (DSLR) — models that follow turn-taking, gaze, and repair, and everything else that happens when signers talk to each other.
